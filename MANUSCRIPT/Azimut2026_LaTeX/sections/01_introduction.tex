\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{The importance of reliable surface-orientation metadata}

In situ measurements of global tilted irradiance (GTI) are essential to estimate the output of photovoltaic systems, and its accuracy is directly related to the economic risk of such projects. Orientation errors in tilted sensors can propagate to irradiance transposition and model validation studies, particularly at unattended sites.

The optimal orientation of PV arrrays in terms of the year-round production is that of the Equator, i.e. due North (South) if the observer is located in the southern (northern) hemisphere. Small misalignments from this orientation can result in uncertainties that may impact in financial risk for PV projects. 

Misalignment of tilted sensors can happen for a variety of reasons: incorrect determination of the true north-south direction, poor fixation of the sensors or extreme weather conditions that may move the platforms where the sensors are located.

Transposition models, that is, determination of GTI from the horizontal global irradiance GHI and assumptions about the diffuse component of radiation is highly dependent of the incidence angle of the direct component  of radiation, which also depends on the surface orientation, given by the tilt and azimuth.

Orientation inference has been investigated using measured irradiance and modeled clear-sky responses \citep{barbier2019,piccioli2021}, and using photovoltaic generation measurements \citep{meng2020}. Our earlier conference paper introduced a novel approach that allows for azimuth estimation for fixed tilt surfaces using only GTI data, and also is capable of detecting changes in azimuth \citep{piccioli2021}. A subsequent work \citep{piccioli2022thesis} applied orientation diagnosis in the context of transposition-model evaluation. The method was validated using GTI ground data with various nominal fixed orientations (both tilt and azimuth), but not enough to fully assess the uncertainty associated to the method.

This work aims to expand this validation to a more refined set of orientations, with a fully controlled set of data.

The remaining challenge is not only to estimate an azimuth, but to determine
when that estimate is trustworthy. Specifically, the relationship between
measurement quality, stability of the fitted GTI maximum, uncertainty in
estimated azimuth, and practical usefulness for unattended stations requires
systematic assessment.

The objectives of this study are to (i) validate the peak-hour-angle approach
against documented surface orientations; (ii) quantify uncertainty and the
reliability of daily GTI fits; (iii) investigate robustness and multi-day
aggregation; and (iv) illustrate its potential for diagnosing orientation
inconsistencies and assessing their impact on transposition-model validation.
